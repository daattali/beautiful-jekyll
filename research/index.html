research

Short tandem repeats are among the most mutation-prone stretches of the human genome. They play a key role in repeat expansion disorders such as Huntington’s disease, Fragile X syndrome, alongside contributing to microsatellite instability linked to 20–30% of colorectal and endometrial cancers. Pedigree analyses demonstrate their high germline mutability linked to somatic instability which can result in mutations in subset of cells which are hard to find.

My work makes these hidden mutations measurable: in single cells, in individual patients, and across whole populations.

Methods · 2024

Finding mosaic repeats without a control sample

prancSTR is the first hypothesis-testing framework for detecting somatic mosaicism at short tandem repeats. It models sequencing reads as a mixture to infer mosaic fractions with formal statistical guarantees.

Applied to 1000 Genomes, it revealed population-specific patterns of mosaic mutation not described before.

Single-cell · in progress

Tracing cell lineages with repeat mutations

Single-cell data is noisy in ways bulk methods were never built for. I’m redesigning prancSTR with specialized statistical model and machine learning that infer jointly across thousands of cells.

With Boston Children’s Hospital, studying how cells evolve in patients with germline mismatch repair deficiency syndromes.

Population genomics · ongoing

Pangenomes that include everyone

Most clinical genomics rests on references drawn from European ancestry. I’m leading pangenome toolkits that combine population-scale genome graphs with biobank data for variant analysis, GWAS and fine-mapping.

Co-leading a collaboration with African researchers to bring underrepresented populations into the reference.

Statistical genetics · next

What makes some genomes more mosaic?

A genome-wide association study of STR mosaicism in UK Biobank and All of Us, to find the genetic determinants of mosaic mutation rates across loci and ancestries.

Completes a framework that runs from single cells to whole populations.

Clinical translation · one possible postdoc direction

From sequence to diagnosis in CANVAS

CANVAS, a progressive ataxia, is caused by repeat expansions in RFC1, and up to 30% of unexplained ataxia cases still have no genetic diagnosis. One direction I envision taking is to start from patient cohorts, working with neurologists and genetic counselors to characterize RFC1 mosaicism in patients.

Long term: an interdisciplinary group at an academic medical center, bridging computational genomics and patient care.
